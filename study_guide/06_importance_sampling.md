# Importance Sampling

## 1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Explain **why** importance sampling is necessary for off-policy learning and what problem it solves.
- Derive the importance sampling ratio from first principles, starting from the statistical identity $E_p[f(X)] = E_q[f(X) \cdot w(X)]$.
- Compute the importance sampling ratio $\rho_{t:T-1}$ for an RL trajectory and explain why transition probabilities cancel out.
- Distinguish between **ordinary importance sampling** and **weighted importance sampling** in terms of bias, variance, and convergence properties.
- Apply both ordinary and weighted IS to estimate value functions from off-policy episodes, including incremental computation.
- Work through numerical examples end-to-end: compute weights, returns, and value estimates for both the statistical and RL settings.

---

## 2. Ground-Up Explanation

### The Fundamental Problem

In on-policy methods (e.g., on-policy Monte Carlo), the agent evaluates and improves the **same** policy it uses to generate experience. But what if we want to learn about a **different** policy than the one generating data?

> [!question] Why would we want off-policy learning?
> - We may want to learn about an **optimal** (greedy) policy while following an **exploratory** policy.
> - We may want to reuse data collected under old policies.
> - We may want to learn from demonstrations or data generated by a human or another agent.

This creates a mismatch: we have samples drawn from a **behavior policy** $b$, but we want to compute expectations under a **target policy** $\pi$. The distributions over trajectories induced by $b$ and $\pi$ are different, so we cannot simply average returns and claim we have estimated $v_\pi(s)$.

### The Core Idea

Importance sampling is a general statistical technique that solves exactly this problem. The idea is deceptively simple:

> [!tip] Core Insight
> If you have samples from the **wrong** distribution, you can **reweight** each sample by how much more (or less) likely it would have been under the **correct** distribution. The ratio $\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ is called the **importance sampling ratio** or **importance weight**.

Samples that are much more likely under the target distribution get amplified. Samples that are much less likely get diminished. On average, this reweighting corrects the distributional mismatch.

### From Statistics to RL

The statistical version: we want $E_p[f(X)]$ but only have samples from $q(X)$.

The RL version: we want $E_\pi[\text{return}]$ but only have episodes generated by $b$.

The mechanism is identical. The importance weight in RL is the product of policy probability ratios along the trajectory.

## 3. Analogies

> [!example] Analogy 1 -- Opinion Poll with Biased Sample
> Suppose you want to estimate the average opinion of an entire city (target distribution $p$), but you can only survey people at a shopping mall (sampling distribution $q$). Mall-goers are not representative of the city. However, if you know **how over- or under-represented** each demographic is in the mall compared to the city, you can reweight each person's response accordingly. A demographic that is twice as common at the mall as in the city gets its responses halved. That reweighting is importance sampling.

> [!example] Analogy 2 -- Currency Conversion
> Think of the importance weight as an exchange rate. You have "returns" denominated in the currency of policy $b$. To convert them into the currency of policy $\pi$, you multiply by the exchange rate $\rho = \pi / b$. If $\pi$ makes a trajectory twice as likely as $b$ does, that trajectory's return is worth twice as much in the $\pi$-currency.

> [!example] Analogy 3 -- Grading on a Curve
> Imagine a teacher collects homework scores from Class B but wants to estimate the average score that Class A would have gotten on the same assignments. If the teacher knows how each student type is distributed across both classes, she can reweight Class B's scores to estimate Class A's average. The reweighting factor for each score is the ratio of how common that student type is in Class A vs. Class B.

## 4. Statistical Foundation of Importance Sampling

### Setup

Let $p(x)$ be the **target distribution** (the one we care about) and $q(x)$ be the **proposal/sampling distribution** (the one we can sample from). We want to estimate:

$$E_p[f(X)] = \int f(x) \, p(x) \, dx$$

but we can only draw samples $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N \sim q(x)$.

### The Key Identity

> [!abstract] Importance Sampling Identity
> $$E_p[f(X)] = \int f(x) \, p(x) \, dx = \int f(x) \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} q(x) \, dx = E_q\!\left[f(X) \cdot \frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right]$$

**Proof (step by step):**

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_p[f(X)] &= \int f(x) \, p(x) \, dx \\
&= \int f(x) \cdot \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \cdot q(x) \, dx \quad \text{(multiply and divide by } q(x)\text{)} \\
&= E_q\!\left[f(X) \cdot w(X)\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

where the **importance weight** is:

$$w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$$

### The IS Estimator

Given samples $x_1, \ldots, x_N \sim q$, the IS estimate is:

$$\hat{E}_p[f(X)] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i) \cdot w(x_i) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i) \cdot \frac{p(x_i)}{q(x_i)}$$

### Coverage Requirement

> [!warning] Coverage Condition
> We **require** $q(x) > 0$ wherever $p(x) > 0$. That is, the proposal distribution must have **support** everywhere the target does. If there is a region where $p(x) > 0$ but $q(x) = 0$, we can never sample from that region and the estimator is invalid. Formally: $p(x) > 0 \implies q(x) > 0$.

## 5. Importance Sampling in RL

### Setup

- **Target policy** $\pi$ -- the policy we want to evaluate (e.g., the greedy policy).
- **Behavior policy** $b$ -- the policy that actually generates episodes (e.g., an $\varepsilon$-greedy policy).
- We require **coverage**: $\pi(a|s) > 0 \implies b(a|s) > 0$ for all $s, a$.

### Probability of a Trajectory

The probability of a trajectory $S_t, A_t, R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}, \ldots, S_T$ under policy $\pi$ is:

$$\Pr\{\text{trajectory} \mid \pi\} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \pi(A_k | S_k) \cdot p(S_{k+1}, R_{k+1} | S_k, A_k)$$

Under behavior policy $b$:

$$\Pr\{\text{trajectory} \mid b\} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} b(A_k | S_k) \cdot p(S_{k+1}, R_{k+1} | S_k, A_k)$$

### The IS Ratio for a Trajectory

$$\rho_{t:T-1} = \frac{\Pr\{\text{trajectory} \mid \pi\}}{\Pr\{\text{trajectory} \mid b\}} = \frac{\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \pi(A_k | S_k) \cdot p(S_{k+1}, R_{k+1} | S_k, A_k)}{\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} b(A_k | S_k) \cdot p(S_{k+1}, R_{k+1} | S_k, A_k)}$$

### Derivation: Why Transition Probabilities Cancel

> [!note] Key Derivation -- Transition Probabilities Cancel
> $$\rho_{t:T-1} = \frac{\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \pi(A_k|S_k) \cdot \cancel{p(S_{k+1}, R_{k+1}|S_k, A_k)}}{\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} b(A_k|S_k) \cdot \cancel{p(S_{k+1}, R_{k+1}|S_k, A_k)}} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(A_k|S_k)}{b(A_k|S_k)}$$
>
> The environment dynamics $p(S_{k+1}, R_{k+1}|S_k, A_k)$ appear identically in the numerator and denominator and **cancel completely**. This is critical because:
> 1. We do **not** need to know the environment model.
> 2. The IS ratio depends **only** on the two policies and the sequence of actions taken.

### Final IS Ratio

$$\boxed{\rho_{t:T-1} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(A_k | S_k)}{b(A_k | S_k)}}$$

> [!tip] Variable Definitions
> - $t$ -- the starting time step
> - $T$ -- the terminal time step of the episode
> - $\pi(A_k|S_k)$ -- probability of action $A_k$ in state $S_k$ under the target policy
> - $b(A_k|S_k)$ -- probability of action $A_k$ in state $S_k$ under the behavior policy

## 6. Ordinary Importance Sampling

### Formula

$$V_{\text{OIS}}(s) = \frac{1}{|{\mathcal{T}(s)}|} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1} \, G_t$$

where:
- $\mathcal{T}(s)$ is the set of all time steps in which state $s$ is visited (across all episodes).
- $|\mathcal{T}(s)|$ is the number of times state $s$ was visited.
- $T(t)$ is the terminal time step of the episode containing time step $t$.
- $G_t$ is the return from time step $t$ onward.
- $\rho_{t:T(t)-1}$ is the IS ratio from time $t$ to the end of that episode.

### Properties

> [!success] Unbiased
> Ordinary IS is an **unbiased** estimator of $v_\pi(s)$. That is, $E[V_{\text{OIS}}(s)] = v_\pi(s)$.

> [!danger] High Variance
> The IS ratios $\rho$ are products of potentially many ratios. If $\pi$ and $b$ differ substantially, these products can become extremely large or extremely small. This leads to **very high variance**, which can even be **infinite** when the ratios are unbounded. A single enormous ratio can dominate the entire estimate, making it unreliable in practice.

---

## 7. Weighted Importance Sampling

### Formula

$$V_{\text{WIS}}(s) = \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1} \, G_t}{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1}}$$

If the denominator is zero (no visits), the value is defined as zero.

### Properties

> [!warning] Biased (But Only Initially)
> Weighted IS is a **biased** estimator. The bias arises because we normalize by the sum of weights (a random quantity) rather than the count. However, the bias is **asymptotically zero** -- it vanishes as the number of samples grows.

> [!success] Lower Variance
> Weighted IS has **dramatically lower variance** than ordinary IS. The normalization by the sum of weights acts as a stabilizer. The variance is bounded even when individual ratios are large. This is why weighted IS is **preferred in practice**.

> [!note] Single-Episode Special Case
> With first-visit MC and only a single episode visiting state $s$, weighted IS always returns $G_t$ regardless of $\rho$ (because $\rho$ cancels in numerator and denominator). This means the first estimate is biased toward $v_b(s)$ rather than $v_\pi(s)$. Ordinary IS, by contrast, gives $\rho \cdot G_t$, which is unbiased but potentially high-variance.

---

## 8. Ordinary vs Weighted IS Comparison

| Property | Ordinary IS | Weighted IS |
|---|---|---|
| **Bias** | Unbiased | Biased (asymptotically unbiased) |
| **Variance** | High (can be infinite) | Lower, bounded |
| **First-visit, single episode** | $E[V] = v_\pi(s)$ exactly | Biased toward $v_b(s)$ |
| **Convergence** | Slower (high variance hurts) | Faster (low variance helps) |
| **Formula denominator** | $\|\mathcal{T}(s)\|$ (count of visits) | $\sum \rho$ (sum of weights) |
| **Preferred in practice?** | Theoretically clean | **Practically preferred** |
| **Sensitivity to large $\rho$** | Very sensitive | Robust |

> [!tip] Rule of Thumb
> Unless you have a specific theoretical reason to need unbiasedness, **use weighted importance sampling**. The variance reduction is almost always worth the small bias.

## 9. Incremental Implementation

For off-policy MC control with weighted IS, we need an efficient incremental update rule. Suppose we have $n$ returns $G_1, G_2, \ldots, G_n$ for a given state (or state-action pair), each with importance weight $W_i$.

### Incremental Update Equations

$$C_{n+1} = C_n + W_{n+1}$$

$$V_{n+1} = V_n + \frac{W_{n+1}}{C_{n+1}} \left(G_{n+1} - V_n\right)$$

where:
- $C_n$ is the **cumulative sum of weights** after $n$ returns (initialized to $C_0 = 0$).
- $V_n$ is the current value estimate after $n$ returns.
- $W_{n+1} = \rho_{t:T(t)-1}$ is the importance weight for the $(n+1)$-th return.
- $G_{n+1}$ is the $(n+1)$-th return.

> [!note] Connection to Weighted Average
> This is mathematically equivalent to computing the weighted IS formula directly, but avoids storing all past returns. It is a **weighted running average** where the step size $\frac{W_{n+1}}{C_{n+1}}$ naturally decreases as more data accumulates.

> [!abstract] Pseudocode Sketch
> ```
> Initialize V(s) arbitrarily, C(s) = 0 for all s
> For each episode generated by b:
>     G = 0, W = 1
>     For t = T-1, T-2, ..., 0:
>         G = gamma * G + R_{t+1}
>         C(S_t) = C(S_t) + W
>         V(S_t) = V(S_t) + (W / C(S_t)) * (G - V(S_t))
>         W = W * pi(A_t|S_t) / b(A_t|S_t)
>         If W == 0: break
> ```

## 10. Worked Examples

### Example 1 -- Statistical Importance Sampling (from slides)

**Problem:** Estimate $E_p[f(X)]$ where $p(x) = \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, $q(x) = \mathcal{N}(1,1)$, and $f(x) = e^{-x^2}$.

**Step 1: Write the probability density functions.**

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2} \qquad q(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(x-1)^2/2}$$

**Step 2: Compute the importance weight $w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$.**

![[is_s4_img1.png|Importance weight derivation]]

$$w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(x-1)^2/2}}$$

The $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ terms cancel:

$$w(x) = \frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{e^{-(x-1)^2/2}} = e^{-x^2/2 + (x-1)^2/2}$$

Expand the exponent:

$$-\frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{(x-1)^2}{2} = \frac{-x^2 + x^2 - 2x + 1}{2} = \frac{-2x + 1}{2} = -x + \frac{1}{2}$$

$$\boxed{w(x) = e^{-x + 1/2}}$$

**Step 3: Draw $N = 5$ samples from $q(x) = \mathcal{N}(1,1)$.**

Samples: $x_1 = 0.5, \; x_2 = 1.2, \; x_3 = 0.8, \; x_4 = 1.5, \; x_5 = 2.0$

**Step 4: Compute function values, importance weights, and weighted values.**

| $x_i$ | $f(x_i) = e^{-x_i^2}$ | $w(x_i) = e^{-x_i + 0.5}$ | $f(x_i) \cdot w(x_i)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | $e^{-0.25} = 0.7788$ | $e^{0.0} = 1.0000$ | 0.7788 |
| 1.2 | $e^{-1.44} = 0.2369$ | $e^{-0.7} = 2.0138$ | 0.4771 |
| 0.8 | $e^{-0.64} = 0.5273$ | $e^{-0.3} = 1.3499$ | 0.7118 |
| 1.5 | $e^{-2.25} = 0.1054$ | $e^{-1.0} = 2.7183$ | 0.2865 |
| 2.0 | $e^{-4.0} = 0.0183$ | $e^{-1.5} = 4.4817$ | 0.0821 |

$$\sum_{i=1}^{5} f(x_i) \cdot w(x_i) = 0.7788 + 0.4771 + 0.7118 + 0.2865 + 0.0821 = 3.170$$

> [!warning] Note on $w(x_i)$ values
> Observe that samples further from the mean of $p$ (i.e., further from 0) get higher weights. The sample at $x = 2.0$ has weight $4.48$ because it is very unlikely under $p$ relative to $q$... wait, actually the opposite: $w(x) = p(x)/q(x)$, so a high weight means the sample is relatively more likely under $p$ than under $q$. At $x = 0.5$, both distributions assign roughly equal density, so $w \approx 1$.

**Step 5: Estimate the expectation.**

![[is_s6_img5.png|IS estimator calculation]]

$$\hat{E}_p[f(X)] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i) \cdot w(x_i) = \frac{1}{5} \times 3.170 = \boxed{0.634}$$

### Example 2 -- RL Episode with Importance Sampling (from slides)

**Problem:** Given an episode under behavior policy $b$, estimate the value using importance sampling for target policy $\pi$.

**Episode:**
$$S_0 = s_1, \; A_0 = a_1, \; R_1 = 2, \; S_1 = s_2, \; A_1 = a_2, \; R_2 = 5$$

**Policies:**

| | $b$ (behavior) | $\pi$ (target) |
|---|---|---|
| $a_1$ given $s_1$ | 0.4 | 0.6 |
| $a_2$ given $s_2$ | 0.3 | 0.5 |

Discount factor: $\gamma = 0.9$.

**Step 1: Compute the return $G$.**

$$G = R_1 + \gamma R_2 = 2 + 0.9 \times 5 = 2 + 4.5 = 6.5$$

**Step 2: Compute the IS ratio $\rho$.**

$$\rho_{0:1} = \prod_{k=0}^{1} \frac{\pi(A_k|S_k)}{b(A_k|S_k)} = \frac{\pi(a_1|s_1)}{b(a_1|s_1)} \cdot \frac{\pi(a_2|s_2)}{b(a_2|s_2)}$$

$$\rho_{0:1} = \frac{0.6}{0.4} \times \frac{0.5}{0.3} = 1.5 \times 1.667 = 2.5$$

**Step 3: Compute the off-policy return estimate.**

$$\hat{V}(s_1) = \rho_{0:1} \times G = 2.5 \times 6.5 = \boxed{16.25}$$

> [!note] Interpretation
> The raw return under $b$ was $6.5$. But the target policy $\pi$ is more likely to produce this trajectory (both actions are more probable under $\pi$), so the importance-weighted estimate is higher: $16.25$. This makes sense because $\pi$ preferentially selects the actions that led to this particular (good) return.

### Example 3 -- Multiple Episodes: Ordinary vs Weighted IS

**Problem:** A simple 2-state MDP with states $\{s_1, s_2\}$ and actions $\{L, R\}$. We want to estimate $V_\pi(s_1)$. Three episodes are collected under behavior policy $b$, all starting from $s_1$. Discount factor $\gamma = 1.0$ (undiscounted).

**Policies:**

| State | Action | $b(a|s)$ | $\pi(a|s)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $s_1$ | $L$ | 0.5 | 0.8 |
| $s_1$ | $R$ | 0.5 | 0.2 |
| $s_2$ | $L$ | 0.5 | 0.7 |
| $s_2$ | $R$ | 0.5 | 0.3 |

**Episode 1:** $S_0=s_1, A_0=L, R_1=1, S_1=s_2, A_1=L, R_2=3$ (terminates)

$$G_1 = 1 + 3 = 4$$

$$\rho_1 = \frac{\pi(L|s_1)}{b(L|s_1)} \cdot \frac{\pi(L|s_2)}{b(L|s_2)} = \frac{0.8}{0.5} \cdot \frac{0.7}{0.5} = 1.6 \times 1.4 = 2.24$$

**Episode 2:** $S_0=s_1, A_0=R, R_1=0, S_1=s_2, A_1=R, R_2=2$ (terminates)

$$G_2 = 0 + 2 = 2$$

$$\rho_2 = \frac{\pi(R|s_1)}{b(R|s_1)} \cdot \frac{\pi(R|s_2)}{b(R|s_2)} = \frac{0.2}{0.5} \cdot \frac{0.3}{0.5} = 0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.24$$

**Episode 3:** $S_0=s_1, A_0=L, R_1=1, S_1=s_2, A_1=R, R_2=1$ (terminates)

$$G_3 = 1 + 1 = 2$$

$$\rho_3 = \frac{\pi(L|s_1)}{b(L|s_1)} \cdot \frac{\pi(R|s_2)}{b(R|s_2)} = \frac{0.8}{0.5} \cdot \frac{0.3}{0.5} = 1.6 \times 0.6 = 0.96$$

**Summary Table:**

| Episode | $G_i$ | $\rho_i$ | $\rho_i \cdot G_i$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | 2.24 | 8.96 |
| 2 | 2 | 0.24 | 0.48 |
| 3 | 2 | 0.96 | 1.92 |

**Ordinary IS Estimate:**

$$V_{\text{OIS}}(s_1) = \frac{1}{3}(8.96 + 0.48 + 1.92) = \frac{11.36}{3} = \boxed{3.787}$$

**Weighted IS Estimate:**

$$V_{\text{WIS}}(s_1) = \frac{8.96 + 0.48 + 1.92}{2.24 + 0.24 + 0.96} = \frac{11.36}{3.44} = \boxed{3.302}$$

> [!note] Variance Comparison
> Notice that Episode 1 has a large IS ratio ($\rho = 2.24$) and dominates the ordinary IS estimate. If we had an episode with $\rho = 20$, it would completely dominate. Weighted IS is more robust: even though Episode 1 gets a large weight, the normalization by $\sum \rho$ prevents any single episode from overwhelming the estimate. In practice, over many trials, the ordinary IS estimate would show much higher variance than the weighted IS estimate.

## 11. Key Formulas

> [!danger] MUST MEMORIZE -- IS Ratio (RL)
> $$\rho_{t:T-1} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(A_k|S_k)}{b(A_k|S_k)}$$
> **Variables:**
> - $\pi(A_k|S_k)$: probability of action $A_k$ in state $S_k$ under target policy
> - $b(A_k|S_k)$: probability of action $A_k$ in state $S_k$ under behavior policy
> - Product runs from current time $t$ to one step before terminal time $T$

> [!danger] MUST MEMORIZE -- Ordinary IS Value Estimate
> $$V_{\text{OIS}}(s) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}(s)|} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1} \, G_t$$
> **Variables:**
> - $\mathcal{T}(s)$: set of time steps where state $s$ is visited
> - $|\mathcal{T}(s)|$: count of visits to $s$
> - $G_t$: return following time step $t$

> [!danger] MUST MEMORIZE -- Weighted IS Value Estimate
> $$V_{\text{WIS}}(s) = \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1} \, G_t}{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1}}$$
> **Key difference from Ordinary IS:** denominator is $\sum \rho$ instead of count $|\mathcal{T}(s)|$.

> [!danger] MUST MEMORIZE -- Incremental Weighted IS Update
> $$C_{n+1} = C_n + W_{n+1}$$
> $$V_{n+1} = V_n + \frac{W_{n+1}}{C_{n+1}}(G_{n+1} - V_n)$$
> **Variables:**
> - $C_n$: cumulative sum of importance weights after $n$ updates
> - $W_{n+1}$: importance weight for the new return ($= \rho$ for that episode)

> [!danger] MUST MEMORIZE -- Statistical IS Identity
> $$E_p[f(X)] = E_q\!\left[f(X) \cdot \frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right]$$

## 12. Derivations

### Derivation 1: Why Transition Probabilities Cancel

The probability of generating trajectory $\tau = (S_t, A_t, R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}, \ldots, S_T)$ starting from $S_t$ under policy $\pi$ is:

$$\Pr(\tau | \pi) = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \underbrace{\pi(A_k|S_k)}_{\text{policy}} \cdot \underbrace{p(S_{k+1}, R_{k+1} | S_k, A_k)}_{\text{environment dynamics}}$$

The IS ratio is:

$$\rho = \frac{\Pr(\tau|\pi)}{\Pr(\tau|b)} = \frac{\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \pi(A_k|S_k) \cdot p(S_{k+1},R_{k+1}|S_k,A_k)}{\prod_{k=t}^{T-1} b(A_k|S_k) \cdot p(S_{k+1},R_{k+1}|S_k,A_k)}$$

Since the environment dynamics $p(S_{k+1},R_{k+1}|S_k,A_k)$ do not depend on the policy, they appear identically in both numerator and denominator for each $k$:

$$\rho = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(A_k|S_k) \cdot p(S_{k+1},R_{k+1}|S_k,A_k)}{b(A_k|S_k) \cdot p(S_{k+1},R_{k+1}|S_k,A_k)} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(A_k|S_k)}{b(A_k|S_k)}$$

> [!tip] Significance
> This cancellation is what makes off-policy learning **practical** in model-free RL. We never need to know or estimate the transition dynamics $p$.

### Derivation 2: Connection Between Ordinary and Weighted IS

Both estimators can be written in a unified form:

$$V(s) = \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1} \, G_t}{\text{normalizer}}$$

- **Ordinary IS:** normalizer $= |\mathcal{T}(s)|$ (the count of visits, a deterministic quantity given the data).
- **Weighted IS:** normalizer $= \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1}$ (a random quantity, since $\rho$ depends on the sampled actions).

The key difference is that weighted IS uses a **data-dependent normalizer**. Since $E[\rho] = 1$ under the behavior policy (by definition of the IS ratio), we have $E\!\left[\sum \rho\right] \approx |\mathcal{T}(s)|$ for large samples. This is why weighted IS is asymptotically unbiased -- its normalizer converges to the same value ordinary IS uses from the start. The bias comes from the fact that $E\!\left[\frac{\sum \rho \cdot G}{\sum \rho}\right] \neq \frac{E[\sum \rho \cdot G]}{E[\sum \rho]}$ for finite samples (expectation of a ratio is not the ratio of expectations).

## 13. Common Mistakes

> [!warning] Common Mistake 1 -- Including Transition Probabilities in the Ratio
> Students sometimes try to include the environment dynamics $p(s'|s,a)$ in the IS ratio. Remember: **transition probabilities cancel**. The IS ratio only involves policy probabilities.

> [!warning] Common Mistake 2 -- Confusing Which Policy Is Which
> The **target** policy $\pi$ goes in the **numerator**. The **behavior** policy $b$ goes in the **denominator**. Getting this backward inverts the correction. Mnemonic: we are converting FROM $b$ TO $\pi$, so $\pi / b$.

> [!warning] Common Mistake 3 -- Forgetting the Product Over Time Steps
> The IS ratio is a **product** over all time steps in the episode, not just a single ratio. Each step contributes one factor $\pi(A_k|S_k)/b(A_k|S_k)$.

> [!warning] Common Mistake 4 -- Confusing Bias/Variance Properties
> Ordinary IS is **unbiased** but high variance. Weighted IS is **biased** but low variance. Do not mix these up. A common exam error is to say weighted IS is unbiased.

> [!warning] Common Mistake 5 -- Ignoring the Coverage Requirement
> If $\pi(a|s) > 0$ but $b(a|s) = 0$ for some state-action pair, importance sampling is **invalid**. The behavior policy must cover all actions that the target policy might take. Violating this means some trajectories under $\pi$ can never be sampled.

> [!warning] Common Mistake 6 -- Applying IS Ratio to Wrong Range
> For estimating $V(s)$ starting from time $t$, the IS ratio runs from $t$ to $T-1$. For estimating $Q(s,a)$ starting from time $t$, the ratio runs from $t+1$ to $T-1$ (the first action is already fixed).

## 14. Self-Check Questions

> [!question]- Why can't we simply average returns from behavior policy $b$ to estimate $v_\pi(s)$?
> Because the distribution of trajectories (and hence returns) under $b$ is different from the distribution under $\pi$. Actions are selected with different probabilities, leading to different state-action sequences. Averaging $b$'s returns gives us $v_b(s)$, not $v_\pi(s)$.

> [!question]- Derive the importance weight for $p = \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $q = \mathcal{N}(1,1)$.
> $w(x) = p(x)/q(x) = e^{-x^2/2} / e^{-(x-1)^2/2} = e^{(-x^2 + (x-1)^2)/2} = e^{(-2x+1)/2} = e^{-x+1/2}$. The normalization constants cancel since both are $\mathcal{N}(\cdot, 1)$ with the same variance.

> [!question]- Why do transition probabilities cancel in the RL importance sampling ratio?
> Because the environment dynamics $p(s', r | s, a)$ are the same regardless of which policy selected the action. They appear identically in the numerator (under $\pi$) and denominator (under $b$) of the trajectory probability ratio, and thus cancel. Only the policy probabilities differ.

> [!question]- If $\pi(a|s) = 0.9$ and $b(a|s) = 0.1$ for every step in a 10-step episode, what is $\rho$?
> $\rho = (0.9/0.1)^{10} = 9^{10} = 3{,}486{,}784{,}401$. This astronomically large ratio illustrates why ordinary IS can have enormous (even infinite) variance. A single episode could dominate the entire estimate.

> [!question]- In what situation does weighted IS give a biased first estimate?
> When there is only a single episode visiting state $s$, the weighted IS estimate reduces to $\frac{\rho \cdot G}{\rho} = G$, which is the raw return under the behavior policy. This estimates $v_b(s)$, not $v_\pi(s)$, so it is biased.

> [!question]- What is the coverage requirement, and why is it necessary?
> Coverage requires $\pi(a|s) > 0 \implies b(a|s) > 0$. If the behavior policy assigns zero probability to an action that the target policy might take, we can never observe that action in our data, and the IS ratio would involve division by zero. We would have no information about trajectories that $\pi$ considers possible.

> [!question]- Write the incremental update equations for weighted IS and explain what each variable represents.
> $C_{n+1} = C_n + W_{n+1}$ (cumulative weight sum); $V_{n+1} = V_n + \frac{W_{n+1}}{C_{n+1}}(G_{n+1} - V_n)$ (value update). $C_n$ tracks the total weight seen so far, $W_{n+1}$ is the IS ratio for the new episode, $G_{n+1}$ is the new return, and $V_n$ is the current value estimate. The fraction $W_{n+1}/C_{n+1}$ acts as a decreasing step size.

> [!question]- Why is weighted IS generally preferred over ordinary IS in practice?
> Despite being biased, weighted IS has much lower variance (bounded vs. potentially infinite). The bias vanishes asymptotically, so with sufficient data both converge to the true value. In practice, the variance reduction of weighted IS leads to faster, more stable convergence.
